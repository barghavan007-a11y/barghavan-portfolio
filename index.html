Harmonium

A harmonium sitting on a patterned rug in an ornate room slowly comes apart. The wood breaks into chunks and dust, and the music it used to make spills out as glowing gold notes and glitter. I wanted the moment to feel like the instrument is playing its last song as it disappears, and I wanted it to look real rather than CG.

The effect is built in Houdini 22: the harmonium is fractured and driven apart with particles, and the notes, dust and glitter are separate particle systems so each could be timed and shaded on its own. The glitter is real bead geometry so it catches light properly. I rendered the beauty and its passes in Redshift through Solaris, rendered depth and ID mattes in Karma, and put it all together in Nuke with depth haze, glow from the notes lighting the air, bloom, film halation, grain and a light vignette.

Software: Houdini (Solaris, particles), Redshift, Karma, Nuke · Format: 1920 × 1080, 24 fps

Moonlit Shrine

A matte painting of a cave shrine lit only by oil lamps and a crescent moon. The work is built in depth layers, from the wet foreground rock through the seated cow and the sanctum to the open night sky beyond the cave mouth, with atmospheric haze used to separate each plane and hold the scale of the space. Lighting is the core of the piece: the warm pools of lamp flame are kept as the only source of heat in an otherwise cool frame, so the eye travels inward to the central figure without needing any hard compositional lines. Colour, value and edge treatment were balanced across the whole frame to keep every element sitting convincingly in the same light.

I then brought the painting into Nuke and split it into depth layers projected onto cards, so a camera can move through the cave and the foreground, shrine and sky shift against each other like a real space. The end of the video shows the 3D setup.

Georgia Wildfire

Forest Fire Simulation Technical Summary

Summary

A ground-level shot of a south Georgia pine stand burning, matched to CBS Mornings news footage of the spring 2026 fires. Two detailed hero pines catch fire from the ground up and torch into the crown while the wind pushes the flames and bends the trees. A low ground fire burns around their base, a forest edge fills the frame behind them, and a layer of smoke hangs over the whole scene. The camera, wind and timing are all matched to the reference clip.

The project started as an aerial shot with Copernicus Pyro (Entry 1). After feedback I switched to DOP pyro, so the fire can interact with the real tree geometry, and to a closer shot built around two hero trees (Entry 2). The latest round added tree variants, split the fire into separate pyro DOPs and matched the layout, camera, embers and ground fire to the clip (Entry 3).

  • Software: Houdini 22 (SOPs, VEX, Sparse Pyro Solver), Nuke
  • Shot: 160 frames at 24 fps (6.7 seconds) to match the reference, 1920 × 1080
  • Main effects: torching crowns, trunk and ground fire, wind-driven tree motion, smoke layers, forest edge

Entry 1 Progress Blog

Goal

Find reference, block out the forest, and get a first fire simulation running in COP Pyro.

References

I collected news and drone footage of the Pineland Road and Highway 82 fires in southeast Georgia. The things I want to match:

  • Tall, thin slash and loblolly pines planted in rows, with bare trunks and all the branches bunched at the top.
  • The ground fire moves as thin glowing ribbons through the pine litter, not as one big wall.
  • Single trees "torch" when the fire climbs into the crown.
  • Dirt roads act as firebreaks, and small spot fires sometimes jump across them.
  • Thick white to grey smoke that leans with the wind.

Process

The shot breaks down into these parts:

  1. Procedural terrain and pine plantation
  2. A fire front that decides where the fire is on every frame
  3. Ground fire line (COP Pyro)
  4. Torching crowns (COP Pyro)
  5. Smoke column
  6. Embers (POPs), stretch goal

Today I focused on 1 to 4.

Which pyro to use?

Houdini 22 has two options: the regular Sparse Pyro solver in DOPs, and the newer COP Pyro that runs on the GPU inside Copernicus. I went with COP Pyro because it sims fast enough to scrub almost in real time on my RTX 5080, which makes look development much quicker, and it is something we did not cover in class, so it is a good chance to learn it. The trade-off is that COP Pyro is less flexible than the DOP solver (fewer ways to add custom microsolvers), so I am keeping Sparse Pyro as a backup if I hit a wall.

Scale

Everything is built at real-world scale in metres. The forest is 300 × 300 m and the trees are 18 to 26 m tall. A small voxel size over an area that big would be far too heavy, so the sim runs at a Voxel Size of 0.5 for now with sparse activation, so only the voxels near the fire are simulated. I also set the block's Time Scale back to 1 (the stock fire setup uses 5 because it is built for a tiny candle-sized flame).

Procedural forest

  • Terrain: a heightfield with Amplitude 2.5 and Element Size 120. South Georgia is very flat, so the relief is under a metre.
  • Pine generator: one VEX wrangle builds the trunk (with a root flare and a few dead stubs), 9 layers of branches at the top, and needle clumps on the outer part of each branch. It outputs 4 tree variants, about 4,300 polygons each.
  • Plantation: trees are placed in rows (4.5 m between rows, 3 m between trees), with 30% randomly missing, a sand road cut through the middle, and clear-cut patches on one side. That gives about 4,000 trees, copied as packed instances.
  • Everything is driven from one CTRL node, so I can change the layout without touching the network.

Fire front

Before simulating anything I needed to control where the fire is on every frame. I made an animated "fire front" attribute on a 1 m ground grid. It moves toward the camera at 2 m/s, and noise breaks the line into fingers instead of a straight edge. Each point gets flame, char, smolder and ember values based on how long ago the fire passed it. Points past the road do not burn, except a few spot fires that jump across about 2.5 seconds in. About 12% of trees are flagged to torch, and their crown fire ramps up and dies down over 4 seconds once the front reaches them.

Sourcing COP Pyro

I started from SideFX's built-in Pyro Configure Fire setup and removed its test tube source. The burning ground points and the torching crowns are rasterized in SOPs into density, temperature, flame and v volumes (Volume Rasterize Attributes, 0.5 m voxels). In Copernicus, a SOP Import and a Geometry to Layer node turn them into VDBs that plug straight into the Pyro Block Begin inputs. On the Pyro Block End I turned on flame sourcing, disturbance, turbulence and a 2.5 m/s wind toward the camera, and lowered density dissipation to 0.05 so the smoke hangs around.

Iterations

The first sim works, but it reads as a row of separate puffs, like small mushroom clouds, instead of one fire line. Each source point makes its own blob, and the flame band is too thin to connect them.

Problems

  • Puffy fire: covered above. The fix is a wider flame band and more turbulence.

To Do

  1. Widen the flame band from 6 m to about 14 m so the fire line connects.
  2. Raise turbulence and disturbance to break up the round plumes.
  3. Lower density dissipation further so a proper smoke column builds.
  4. Drop the voxel size to 0.25 m once the look is right.
  5. Embers with POPs, driven by the sim velocity.
  6. Burnt bark shader for the trees, then lighting and an EXR render.

Progress

Process

The shot is built from four layers that are put together in one scene:

1. The two hero trees

  • Both pines are built in an Attribute Wrangle (VEX): trunk, branches, needle clumps and a few dead twigs.
  • Every polygon gets a kind attribute (trunk, branch, needles or dead twig), so each part can burn differently.
  • All the main settings (tree positions, burn times, wind) live on one CTRL Null node, so I can change things in one place.

2. Making the trees burn

  • A primitive wrangle (BURN_TREES) works out when each part of the tree catches fire: first the ground fire reaches the tree, then the flames climb the trunk, then the whole crown catches at once.
  • Each part gets a flame, char and ember value over time. Needles burn for about 12 s, branches for 14 s and the trunk for 16 s.
  • Burnt needles are deleted, so the crown thins out as it burns, and burnt wood turns black.
  • A second wrangle (FIRE_SOURCES) scatters points on the burning parts with density, temperature, flame and v (velocity).
  • Volume Rasterize Attributes turns those points into volumes, which feed the Pyro Solver.
  • The trunks and branches are turned into a collision volume, so the fire flows around the wood instead of through it.

3. Matching the wind

  • Measuring the reference: I wrote a small Python script that finds the bright flame pixels in each frame of the reference and measures how far the flame leans sideways from bottom to top. That gives one lean value per frame.
  • Wind curve in Houdini: I turned the lean values into a wind speed and keyed them onto a wind_speed channel on the CTRL node, one key per frame. That curve is the wind for the whole shot.
  • Pyro Solver wind: the Wind Strength parameter on the Pyro Solver reads that curve with the expression chf("../CTRL/wind_speed", $F + 4) * 4.0.
    • $F + 4 reads the wind 4 frames early, because a fire takes a moment to react to a gust.
    • * 4.0 scales it up so the lean in the sim matches the reference.
  • Source velocity: the fire source points also get a sideways velocity from wind_speed, so the flames lean right from where they start, not only higher up.
  • Buoyancy: I lowered Buoyancy Lift to 0.7 so the wind can push the flames over instead of them shooting straight up.
  • Checking it: I measured the flame lean in my flipbook the same way and compared it to the reference frame by frame. The two curves line up closely.
  • The same wind_speed curve also drives the tree motion, the smoke layer and the drift of the ground fire, so everything moves with one wind.

4. Moving the trees with the wind

  • My first version just made the trees shake randomly, which looked wrong.
  • I rebuilt it in a point wrangle (TREE_SWAY) that reads the same wind_speed channel.
  • Lean: the trees lean further when the wind is stronger (lean grows with wind speed squared).
  • Gusts: a noise pattern moves through the scene at 3 m/s in the wind direction, so gusts roll from one tree to the next instead of hitting both at once.
  • Bend: the bend is multiplied by height squared, so the base stays planted and the top moves the most.
  • Detail: branches get an extra push from local gusts, and the needles get a tiny rustle.
  • The lean, sway and flutter amounts are sliders on the CTRL node.

5. Ground fire

  • A wrangle on a ground grid (GROUND_FIRE) adds a band of fire around the base of the trees.
  • Noise breaks the band into patches, and the noise drifts with the wind so the patches move.
  • The first pass was much too wide. I limited it to about 15 m across, around the two trees, to match the reference.
  • The ground fire runs hotter and lifts harder than the first version, so it stands up like the base fire in the clip.

6. Overall smoke, smoke on the left and smoke colour

  • The main fire sim only covers the two trees, so the haze over the whole scene is a second Pyro Solver with bigger voxels (0.3 m), which keeps it light.
  • It starts at frame -95, so the smoke is already in the air when the shot begins.
  • It has three sources, merged together:
    • a wide band of soft smoke behind the trees,
    • a second source on the left that starts off screen and higher up, which the wind carries into the frame,
    • some of the smoke coming off the hero fire.
  • Dissipation is very low, so the smoke builds into a haze instead of separate puffs.
  • Colour: on both Pyro Solvers I set the smoke colour to a fire-lit orange and the shadow colour to a warm brown, instead of grey and black.
  • The smoke in the reference is really grey smoke lit by the fire, so in the final render I want to light it with the fire instead.

7. Flame shape

  • The flames were big round blobs that kept the same shape all the time.
  • I added a wrangle (SOURCE_FLICKER) that runs moving noise through the fire sources, so the fire comes out in bursts instead of a steady stream.
  • On the Pyro Solver:
    • Flame Lifespan down to 0.7, so the tips break off.
    • Disturbance and Shredding block size up to 0.45, for bigger break-up shapes.
    • Turbulence swirl size up to 2.5, for larger rolling curls.
  • The flames now lick up in separate tongues and change shape every frame.

8. Trees on a line with point jitter

  • Blast keeps just one hero tree, and a wrangle moves it to the origin.
  • A wrangle gives it a near-black colour, so it reads as a silhouette against the fire.
  • Another wrangle (TREE_LINE_PTS) makes three rows of points behind the hero trees, each row further back.
  • Each point is moved a little at random along the row and in depth (point jitter), so it doesn't look like a fence.
  • Each point gets a random size (pscale 0.85 to 1.15) and a random rotation (orient).
  • No points within 3.5 m of the hero trees.
  • Copy to Points puts the tree on every point as a packed copy, which keeps the viewport fast. That makes 98 trees.

9. Camera and layout

  • The reference is filmed from far away with a long lens, which squashes the trees together.
  • I moved the camera about 170 m back at eye height (1.8 m) and set the focal length to 85 mm.
  • I kept the small tilt and pan I measured from the reference as keyframes on the camera.
  • The ground line now sits about two thirds of the way down, with the fire just right of centre, like the reference.

To Do

  1. Fire along the background trees and a glow along the ground, like the reference.
  2. Trees packed closer together, with a mix of shapes and some shorter, bushier trees.
  3. Light the smoke and trees with the fire in the render.
  4. Match the full length of the reference.
  5. Render and composite in Nuke.

    • Left bank 0.4 → 0.8
    • Smoke fed from the fire 0.3 → 0.45

  • Smoke colour (0.85, 0.42, 0.16) → (0.78, 0.33, 0.07); shadow colour (0.28, 0.08, 0.02) → (0.30, 0.08, 0.01).
  • Camera background: sky gradient sampled from the reference frame.

3. Tree variants

  • Each variant is a copy of the tree network with its own values:
    • Hero pines: crown 32% of the height, 16 whorls, branches 2.6 m, heights 22 m and 25 m.
    • Pine A: crown 60%, 11 whorls, 22 m.
    • Pine B: crown 70%, 8 whorls, short 1.8 m branches.
    • Pine C: crown 50%, 9 whorls, long 3.1 m branches, 20 m.
    • Bushy pine: 10 m, along the front edge only.
    • Round tree: 6.5 m trunk, crown from 4 overlapping spheres with 900 small leaf clumps (Sphere → Scatter → Copy to Points).
  • Attribute Create tags each variant with a variant number; Copy to Points (piece attribute variant) puts the right tree on each point.

4. Grey texture

  • Turned on UVs on every Sweep (trunks, branches, stubs).
  • Attribute from Map reads the bark image through those UVs and bakes it onto the trunk as colour.
  • Attribute Promote moves that colour onto the polygons, so it doesn't override the crown colour.
  • Color nodes: bark grey, crowns darker grey-green 0.14 (before: near-black 0.035).
  • One matte material that uses the geometry colour, so the viewport flipbook shows it reliably.

5. Separate pyro DOPs

  • The fire source points get a src attribute: 0 for tree fire, 1 for floor fire.
  • Two Blast nodes split them: TREE_FIRE_POINTS and GROUND_FIRE_POINTS.
  • Each set goes through Volume Rasterize Attributes into its own Pyro Solver:
    • PYRO_TREES_DOP: voxel 0.08 m, starts at frame -47, collides with the trunks.
    • PYRO_GROUND_DOP: voxel 0.08 → 0.12 m for the wider area, flame lifespan 0.7 → 0.6, buoyancy 0.85 → 0.8, max size 10 m → 120 × 40 × 40 m.
    • PYRO_EMBERS_DOP: voxel 0.04 m for small tongues, starts at frame -60, flame lifespan 0.6, buoyancy 1.2, dissipation 0.2, wind × 1.2.
    • SMOKE_LAYER_DOP: voxel 0.3 m, starts at frame -95.
  • A Merge (PYRO_MERGE) puts the three fires back together for display.
  • All of them read the same wind_speed channel, so they lean together.

6. Ground fire and fire wall

  • Ground grid widened from 40 m to 90 m, so it covers both frame edges.
  • Fire line: a ragged strip about 4 m deep at the front of the tree line, creeping toward the camera at 0.12 m/s; noise breaks it into patches that drift with the wind.
  • Right side burns at 35% strength, because the clip is mostly smoke there.
  • Fire wall: extra source points between the trunks around the main fire, up to about 8 m high, brightest near the ground.
  • Wall source points 6000 → 3500 per frame, heat 1.3 → 0.8, upward speed 2.5–4.5 → 1.2–2.2 m/s.
  • Floor fire flickers patch by patch, separately from the hero crowns.

7. Embers matched to the reference

  • Embers only appear in the bands where they are in the clip:
    • far left (0–18%): 60% of trees
    • left of centre (24–28%): 70%
    • middle (36–42%): 60%

  • Fire frozen for the first 5 s: the sims were reset while the timeline sat on a late frame, so the cache dropped the early frames. Now every sim is reset at frame 1, and the cache limit went from 5000 to 12000 MB.
  • Green crowns in the flipbook: the viewport drew a stray copy of the hero trees when flipbooking from inside the geometry network. Flipbooks now run from object level.
  • Hero crowns: the left hero tree shared its shape with a background variant; it now has its own copy, so the background changes don't affect it.
