Harmonium

A harmonium sitting on a patterned rug in an ornate room slowly comes apart. The wood breaks into chunks and dust, and the music it used to make spills out as glowing gold notes and glitter. I wanted the moment to feel like the instrument is playing its last song as it disappears, and I wanted it to look real rather than CG.

The effect is built in Houdini 22: the harmonium is fractured and driven apart with particles, and the notes, dust and glitter are separate particle systems so each could be timed and shaded on its own. The glitter is real bead geometry so it catches light properly. I rendered the beauty and its passes in Redshift through Solaris, rendered depth and ID mattes in Karma, and put it all together in Nuke with depth haze, glow from the notes lighting the air, bloom, film halation, grain and a light vignette.

Software: Houdini (Solaris, particles), Redshift, Karma, Nuke · Format: 1920 × 1080, 24 fps

Moonlit Shrine

A matte painting of a cave shrine lit only by oil lamps and a crescent moon. The work is built in depth layers, from the wet foreground rock through the seated cow and the sanctum to the open night sky beyond the cave mouth, with atmospheric haze used to separate each plane and hold the scale of the space. Lighting is the core of the piece: the warm pools of lamp flame are kept as the only source of heat in an otherwise cool frame, so the eye travels inward to the central figure without needing any hard compositional lines. Colour, value and edge treatment were balanced across the whole frame to keep every element sitting convincingly in the same light.

I then brought the painting into Nuke and split it into depth layers projected onto cards, so a camera can move through the cave and the foreground, shrine and sky shift against each other like a real space. The end of the video shows the 3D setup.

Georgia Wildfire

Forest Fire Simulation Technical Summary

Summary

A ground-level shot of a south Georgia pine stand burning, matched to CBS Mornings news footage of the spring 2026 fires. Two detailed hero pines catch fire from the ground up and torch into the crown while the wind pushes the flames and bends the trees. A low ground fire burns around their base, a forest edge fills the frame behind them, and a layer of smoke hangs over the whole scene. The camera, wind and timing are all matched to the reference clip.

The project started as an aerial shot with Copernicus Pyro (Entry 1). After feedback I switched to DOP pyro, so the fire can interact with the real tree geometry, and to a closer shot built around two hero trees (Entries 2 and 3).

  • Software: Houdini 22 (SOPs, VEX, Sparse Pyro Solver), Nuke
  • Shot: about 5.3 seconds at 29.97 fps to match the reference, 1920 × 1080
  • Main effects: torching crowns, trunk and ground fire, wind-driven tree motion, smoke layers, forest edge

Georgia Wildfire · Entry 1 · Oct 27, 2026

Entry 1 Progress Blog

Goal

Find reference, block out the forest, and get a first fire simulation running in COP Pyro.

References

I collected news and drone footage of the Pineland Road and Highway 82 fires in southeast Georgia. The things I want to match:

  • Tall, thin slash and loblolly pines planted in rows, with bare trunks and all the branches bunched at the top.
  • The ground fire moves as thin glowing ribbons through the pine litter, not as one big wall.
  • Single trees "torch" when the fire climbs into the crown.
  • Dirt roads act as firebreaks, and small spot fires sometimes jump across them.
  • Thick white to grey smoke that leans with the wind.

Process

The shot breaks down into these parts:

  1. Procedural terrain and pine plantation
  2. A fire front that decides where the fire is on every frame
  3. Ground fire line (COP Pyro)
  4. Torching crowns (COP Pyro)
  5. Smoke column
  6. Embers (POPs), stretch goal

Today I focused on 1 to 4.

Which pyro to use?

Houdini 22 has two options: the regular Sparse Pyro solver in DOPs, and the newer COP Pyro that runs on the GPU inside Copernicus. I went with COP Pyro because it sims fast enough to scrub almost in real time on my RTX 5080, which makes look development much quicker, and it is something we did not cover in class, so it is a good chance to learn it. The trade-off is that COP Pyro is less flexible than the DOP solver (fewer ways to add custom microsolvers), so I am keeping Sparse Pyro as a backup if I hit a wall.

Scale

Everything is built at real-world scale in metres. The forest is 300 × 300 m and the trees are 18 to 26 m tall. A small voxel size over an area that big would be far too heavy, so the sim runs at a Voxel Size of 0.5 for now with sparse activation, so only the voxels near the fire are simulated. I also set the block's Time Scale back to 1 (the stock fire setup uses 5 because it is built for a tiny candle-sized flame).

Procedural forest

  • Terrain: a heightfield with Amplitude 2.5 and Element Size 120. South Georgia is very flat, so the relief is under a metre.
  • Pine generator: one VEX wrangle builds the trunk (with a root flare and a few dead stubs), 9 layers of branches at the top, and needle clumps on the outer part of each branch. It outputs 4 tree variants, about 4,300 polygons each.
  • Plantation: trees are placed in rows (4.5 m between rows, 3 m between trees), with 30% randomly missing, a sand road cut through the middle, and clear-cut patches on one side. That gives about 4,000 trees, copied as packed instances.
  • Everything is driven from one CTRL node, so I can change the layout without touching the network.

Fire front

Before simulating anything I needed to control where the fire is on every frame. I made an animated "fire front" attribute on a 1 m ground grid. It moves toward the camera at 2 m/s, and noise breaks the line into fingers instead of a straight edge. Each point gets flame, char, smolder and ember values based on how long ago the fire passed it. Points past the road do not burn, except a few spot fires that jump across about 2.5 seconds in. About 12% of trees are flagged to torch, and their crown fire ramps up and dies down over 4 seconds once the front reaches them.

Sourcing COP Pyro

I started from SideFX's built-in Pyro Configure Fire setup and removed its test tube source. The burning ground points and the torching crowns are rasterized in SOPs into density, temperature, flame and v volumes (Volume Rasterize Attributes, 0.5 m voxels). In Copernicus, a SOP Import and a Geometry to Layer node turn them into VDBs that plug straight into the Pyro Block Begin inputs. On the Pyro Block End I turned on flame sourcing, disturbance, turbulence and a 2.5 m/s wind toward the camera, and lowered density dissipation to 0.05 so the smoke hangs around.

Iterations

The first sim works, but it reads as a row of separate puffs, like small mushroom clouds, instead of one fire line. Each source point makes its own blob, and the flame band is too thin to connect them.

Problems

  • Puffy fire: covered above. The fix is a wider flame band and more turbulence.

To Do

  1. Widen the flame band from 6 m to about 14 m so the fire line connects.
  2. Raise turbulence and disturbance to break up the round plumes.
  3. Lower density dissipation further so a proper smoke column builds.
  4. Drop the voxel size to 0.25 m once the look is right.
  5. Embers with POPs, driven by the sim velocity.
  6. Burnt bark shader for the trees, then lighting and an EXR render.

Georgia Wildfire · Entry 2 · Oct 28, 2026

Entry 2 Progress Blog

Goal

Rebuild the shot in DOP pyro around two detailed hero trees, match the camera, wind and timing to one reference clip, then work through feedback on the ground fire and the smoke.

Change of Direction

After Entry 1 I got two notes. First, COP Pyro can't interact with real tree geometry, so the trees would never look like they are actually burning. Second, an aerial shot of a whole plantation is too ambitious for the time I have. So I switched to the DOP Sparse Pyro solver and started again with two highly detailed hero trees.

Reference

The things I want to match from this clip:

  • Tall, bare trunks with the crowns torching at the top.
  • Flames that lean hard with the wind and change shape every frame.
  • A long, low camera with a slightly compressed look, with the horizon about two thirds of the way down the frame.
  • Orange smoke that covers the whole sky, lit by the fire from below.

Process

Hero trees

  • Both pines are built in one VEX wrangle. Every polygon is tagged with a kind: trunk, branch, needles or dead twig. The two trees have about 140,000 polygons in total, and most of them are needles.
  • A BURN_TREES primitive wrangle works out when each piece catches fire: when the ground fire reaches the tree, then a climb time up the trunk, then a fast crown time. Dead twigs catch first.
  • Each piece gets flame, char and ember values. Needles burn for 12 s, branches for 14 s and the trunk for 16 s. Burnt needles are removed, so the crown thins out as it burns.
  • The burning pieces scatter source points, which are rasterized into density, temperature, flame and v volumes for the Pyro Solver. The trunks and branches are also turned into a collision SDF, so the fire wraps around the wood instead of passing through it.

Matching the wind

I measured how far the flames lean in every frame of the reference and keyed that onto a wind_speed channel on my CTRL node. The solver's wind reads that channel 4 frames ahead (chf("../CTRL/wind_speed", $F + 4) * 4.0), because the fire needs a moment to react to a gust. I lowered buoyancy lift to 0.7 so the wind can actually bend the flames over. Comparing the flame lean frame by frame, my sim matched the reference with a correlation of about 0.9.

Tree motion

The first version of the tree motion just shook: a random wobble that had nothing to do with the wind. I rebuilt it in a TREE_SWAY wrangle so the trees lean the way the wind is pushing:

  • A steady lean that grows with wind speed squared.
  • Gusts that travel downwind at 3 m/s, so the trees lean one after another instead of all together.
  • The bend grows with height squared, so the base stays planted and the crown moves the most.
  • On top of that, a branch push from local gusts and a small needle rustle.

Feedback round 1: the lower fire and the smoke

The first note was to focus on the lower part of the shot, where the pyro sits at ground level, and on an overall smoke layer. I added a ground fire wall around the base of the trees. Noise breaks it into patches, and the patches drift with the wind. It burns hotter and lifts harder than the thin line from the first sim.

Feedback round 2: too wide

The next note was that the bottom pyro was too wide compared to the reference. I limited the wall to about 15 m across and 7 m deep around the hero trees. My first pass at that cut the fire down too much, so I brought the temperature and lift back up until it stood about as tall as the base fire in the clip.

Smoke layer

The hero sim only covers the two trees, so the smoke that clouds the whole scene is a second, low-resolution pyro sim at 0.3 m voxels. It starts at frame -95, so the smoke is already hanging in the air on frame 1. It is fed by a wide band of soft sources behind the trees and by the smoke coming off the hero fire. Dissipation is very low (0.01), so it builds into a haze instead of separate puffs.

Problems

  • Flames frozen for the first 3.5 seconds: the sim was playing back an old cache after I changed things upstream. Pressing Resimulate on the solver fixed it, and I now do that after every change.
  • Trees shaking instead of bending: fixed with the wind-driven motion above.

To Do

  1. Smoke colour closer to the reference.
  2. Smoke on the left side of the frame.
  3. Better flame shape.
  4. Dress the scene with more trees and match the camera framing.

Georgia Wildfire · Entry 3 · Oct 29, 2026

Entry 3 Progress Blog

Goal

Work through the next round of feedback: another smoke layer on the left, smoke colour closer to the reference, flame shape, and copying trees along a line with point jitter to start dressing the scene. Then match the camera and layout to the clip.

Process

Second smoke layer on the left

I added a second set of sources to the smoke sim on the left of frame. It starts off screen and higher up than the first band, and the wind carries it across the frame. My first pass left the top left corner empty, so I extended it up to about 38 m. Later I thinned it out (density 0.12, dissipation 0.03) because it was piling up too thick.

Smoke colour

In the reference the smoke isn't really orange. It is grey smoke lit by the fire underneath it. For now I'm faking that in the viewport with a fire-lit smoke colour (0.85, 0.42, 0.16) and a warm brown shadow colour instead of a dark one, on both smoke sims. The proper version will be lighting from the fire in the final render.

Flame shape

I wanted separate, licking tongues instead of big round blobs on the crowns. I made the crown sources sparser, made the flames rise faster, and made the break-up noise larger so the tongues split apart and the tips detach.

Tree line

For the forest edge I took one hero tree, moved it to the origin, gave it a near-black colour so it reads as a silhouette against the fire, and copied it onto points with Copy to Points (packed, to keep the viewport light):

  • 3 staggered rows, with trees 3.6 to 4.8 m apart and each row further back than the last.
  • Random jitter along the row and in depth, so it reads as a forest edge rather than a fence.
  • Size between 85% and 115% and a random rotation around Y for each tree.
  • No trees within 3.5 m of the hero trees. 98 trees in total.

Camera and layout

The reference is shot from far away on a long lens, which flattens the tree line. I moved the camera back to about 170 m at eye height (1.8 m) with an 85 mm lens, looking slightly up. I re-based the camera's rotation keys so it keeps the small tilt and pan I measured from the clip. The horizon now sits about two thirds of the way down and the burning trees are just right of centre, like the reference.

The fire looked frozen

With the new camera the sim was technically moving, but the flames held one shape the whole time. The sources were giving off the same amount of fire in the same places every frame, so the fire settled into a steady state. I added a SOURCE_FLICKER wrangle that runs noise up through the flames and downwind. That makes the fire come out in bursts, and each burst gets an extra upward kick. On the solver I shortened the flame lifespan to 0.7, enlarged the disturbance and shredding blocks to 0.45 and the swirl size to 2.5, and raised turbulence. Each frame now changes about 40% more than before, and the flames surge and lick like the clip.

Viewport artifacts

The flipbook had three artifacts. None of them came from the sim; they all came from how the viewport draws the scene:

  • Black patch top left: the smoke thinned out there and the black viewport background showed through. I gave the camera a background plate in the haze colour, so a thin spot now reads as sky.
  • Pale trunks: the fire was lighting the hero trunks in the viewport and blowing them out to white. A black, non-reflective material on the trunks keeps them as dark silhouettes.
  • Thin lines across the sky: the viewport was in Smooth Wire mode. Plain Smooth shading removed them.

To Do

  1. Fire along the background tree line and a glow along the ground, like the reference.
  2. Closer tree spacing and a mix of tree shapes, including some shorter, bushier ones.
  3. Light the smoke and trees with the fire in the render instead of faking it with smoke colour.
  4. Extend the shot to the full length of the reference.
  5. Render EXRs and comp in Nuke.
